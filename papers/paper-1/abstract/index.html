From Movement Organization to Whole-Organism Adaptation

An Integrated Framework for Multisystem Physiological Research

ABSTRACT

From movement organization to adaptation

Regular physical activity is an established component of the prevention and management of noncommunicable diseases, while contemporary systems and Network Physiology approaches investigate coordinated responses across multiple physiological systems and time scales.

However, physical activity is commonly characterized by type, frequency, duration and intensity, while movement research often evaluates specific functional, biomechanical or physiological outcomes. These approaches provide important information but do not necessarily provide an integrated framework for examining how the organization of a movement stimulus relates to its multisystem response and subsequent adaptive trajectory.

The present conceptual study develops an integrated research framework for investigating movement as both an observable expression of ongoing whole-organism organization and a recurrent biological perturbation capable of modifying the internal state of the organism.

The framework distinguishes six analytically related levels: current biological state, movement impulse, organization of movement, multisystem response, recovery/remodelling and adaptation.

These processes are treated as dynamic and recursive rather than strictly linear. Movement quality is not defined as a universally prescribed biomechanical ideal. Instead, it is treated as a multidimensional and context-dependent set of observable characteristics whose biological significance must be evaluated in relation to systemic response and adaptation.

KEY CONCEPTS

Conceptual scope

01

Movement organization

The temporal, spatial, mechanical and neuromuscular organization of a movement episode.

CORE CONSTRUCT
02

Multisystem response

A temporally characterized physiological response involving prespecified variables from more than one biological domain.

RESPONSE LEVEL
03

Adaptation

Reproducible changes across repeated exposure rather than a conclusion based on a single observation.
